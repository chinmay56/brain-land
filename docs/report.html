Section 1

Why a Record of Rights cannot be plotted

Almost every team approaching this problem statement assumes the pipeline is scan the 7/12 → OCR it → put it on a map. That pipeline cannot work, and it is worth being precise about why.

A Record of Rights is a fiscal document. Its job is to record who pays revenue on what, and it does that with a survey number, an owner, an area and a land classification. It has never carried coordinates, because the parcel's shape was recorded separately by the survey establishment, in the tippan (Maharashtra, Karnataka) or the Field Measurement Book (Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh). Two documents, two custodians, one survey number joining them.

Practical consequence for the extraction plan

If the OCR effort targets only the 7/12 / jamabandi corpus, the system will produce a perfect searchable text database and zero map coverage. The tippan and FMB corpus — different shelves, different registers, often a different office — has to be in scope from the start, and it is harder: it is a hand-drawn diagram with a numeric table attached, not a ruled text table.

Tier B is the only source that validates itself: a closed boundary walk must return to its start, and the distance by which it fails is a direct measure of transcription quality. Tier D is the only source present on essentially every document, and although it gives nothing alone, across a village it becomes the constraint network that positions everything (Section 6).

Section 2

What must be extracted, exactly

This is the contract the OCR stage has to satisfy. Anything short of it produces a record that can be searched but not mapped.

2.1 Mandatory on every document, whatever its type

FieldTypeWhy the geometry layer needs it

village, tehsil,

2.2 Then whichever geometry source the document carries

Tier A — Coordinates

Found in: resurvey / DGPS records, SVAMITVA property cards, modern FMB sheets, deed schedules that quote coordinates.

coordinates.points[]Boundary vertices in order, as [x, y] pairs.
coordinates.crsNot optional. "WGS84", a UTM zone, or a full India zone name. See Section 5.2.
coordinates.orderxy or yx — whether the sheet writes long,lat or lat,long.

Tier B — Traverse

Found in: Field Measurement Book, tippan, survey field books, deed schedules given as bearings and distances.

traverse.legs[].bearingWhole circle (125-30-00) or quadrantal (N45°30'E). Both notations must round-trip.
traverse.legs[].distanceNumeric.
traverse.legs[].unitlinks and chains are the usual units in old books; also m, ft, karam, gaz.
magnetic_declination_degOnly if the bearings are magnetic. Omitting it when they are rotates the whole parcel.

Tier C — Chain and offset (the tippan "ladder")

Found in: tippan sketches, village measurement books. This is the commonest geometric record in the legacy corpus and the hardest to extract, because it is a drawing with numbers on it rather than a table.

chain_offset.base_lengthLength of the base line the surveyor ran across the field.
offsets[].chainageDistance along the base line. May be negative — a corner can lie behind the starting station.
offsets[].offsetPerpendicular distance to the boundary point.
offsets[].sideL or R of the base line. Getting this wrong mirrors the parcel.
offsets[].seqCorner order, if the sketch numbers its points. Removes all ambiguity in ring construction.
base_bearingTrue bearing of the base line, if stated. Without it the shape is exact but its orientation is unknown.

Tier D — Four boundaries

boundaries.north / south / east / west, captured as raw text. The solver distinguishes a parcel reference ("Survey No. 125", "S.No 124/2/A") from a non-parcel edge ("Nala", "Village road", "Government land", "Kaluva") and uses the former as constraints. Non-parcel edges are retained, not discarded — they tell you the parcel is on the edge of the block.

2.3 Optional, but each one materially improves the result

control_points[]Any corner whose real position is known: a trijunction pillar, a GTS benchmark, or simply two corners an officer clicks on satellite imagery. Two of them fix position, rotation and scale simultaneously.
parent_survey_noEnables the subdivision-sum check — children must tile the parent.
village_map_sheet_noWhich cadastral sheet the tippan came from; groups parcels for batch assembly.
survey_dateMagnetic declination drifts over decades. An 1890 compass bearing and a 1970 one need different corrections.

The machine-readable version of this whole section is served live at GET /api/gis/requirements, so the extraction team can code against it rather than against a document.

Section 3

The OCR → GIS handoff, in detail

The geometry engine and the OCR stage are deliberately decoupled by a single JSON contract. The engine has never seen an image and never will; OCR never has to know what a Lambert Conformal Conic projection is. This is why the GIS layer is finished while OCR has not started.

3.2 The eight rules the extraction stage must follow

#RuleReason
1Transcribe, do not interpretSend "N45°30'E", not 45.5. The engine parses both notations and records which was on the page.
2Never convert unitsSend the number and the unit written. Unit conversion is state-dependent and the engine refuses unsafe cases rather than guessing.
3Never repair a measurementA leg that looks wrong is the finding. Closure analysis detects it; silently fixing it destroys the detector.
4Preserve order and sequenceLegs in traverse order; offsets with seq when the sketch numbers corners. Reordering changes the polygon.
5State the CRS or omit itA guessed CRS is worse than none. Section 5.2 quantifies this at 195 km.
6Send all four boundaries verbatimIncluding "Nala" and "Village road". Non-parcel edges are information, not noise.
7Emit per-field confidenceRoutes low-confidence fields to officer review before geometry is attempted.
8Batch by village, not by pageThe adjacency solver needs the whole village at once; parcels georeference each other.

3.3 What happens to a document that fails

Nothing is silently dropped. A document that carries no usable source still returns a record, with method: "none", an accuracy class, and a flag naming exactly what is missing and what to do about it — for example "The document carries no coordinates, no traverse, no chain-and-offset measurements and no boundary description. Locate the tippan / Field Measurement Book for this survey number — the textual extract alone never contains geometry." That flag is the work queue for the record room.

Section 5

How the engine works

5.1 Traverse reconstruction and the closure check

A traverse is a walk round the boundary: from each corner, a bearing and a distance to the next. Each leg becomes a displacement — ΔE = L·sin(θ), ΔN = L·cos(θ) — and the walk should return to where it began. It never quite does. The distance by which it fails is the misclosure, and it is the single most valuable number in the whole document, because it detects transcription errors before anything is plotted.

5.2 Coordinate reference systems — why this is not a detail

Legacy Indian cadastral sheets are not on WGS84. They are on Everest 1830 / Kalianpur datums, in the Survey of India's India zones — Lambert Conformal Conic bands with standard parallels at 39°30′, 32°30′, 26°, 19° and 12° N.

The cost of getting it wrong, measured

A parcel at Pune (18.5089 N, 73.9259 E) on Kalianpur 1975 / India zone IIIa has easting 2,102,832 m, northing 871,056 m.

Read correctly18.5089 N, 73.9259 E — Pune
Read as UTM 43N / WGS847.6345 N, 89.3833 E — the Bay of Bengal
Read on the wrong Kalianpur datum195 km away

The engine builds its CRS registry from pyproj's EPSG database at import rather than hard-coding it, so it cannot drift from the authority. It covers all 14 India zones EPSG publishes, all six UTM zones spanning the country (42N–47N), and EPSG:7755 (WGS 84 / India NSF LCC) for national work. It selects the correct UTM zone per parcel from that parcel's own longitude, and can infer which legacy zone an undated sheet most likely used from the EPSG bounding boxes. Round-trip fidelity measured across Pune, Delhi, Kolkata and Madurai is 0.2–1.1 mm. It refuses an ambiguous name: "India zone IIIa" exists on both Kalianpur 1880 and 1975 and they are hundreds of metres apart.

Section 6

How a village assembles itself

This is the part that makes "everything from the documents, nothing external" actually work. A tippan gives an exact shape but no position. A 7/12 gives an exact area and the four boundaries but no shape. Neither is plottable alone — but across a village they form a constraint network, and one anchored parcel places all of them.

6.1 The cost function

Placement is found by minimising a cost, expressed entirely in square metres so the terms are commensurable:

TermRationale
Overlap × 12Two parcels cannot occupy the same ground. Weighted heavily because a placement asserting they do is simply wrong.
Gap²The record says these parcels touch; a gap is a violation.
(1 − cos θ) × L² × 0.5Directional term. If the record says the neighbour is west, the parcel must sit east of it.
− shared edge fraction × L² × 0.30Reward. Adjoining parcels do not merely touch at a point; they share a whole edge because one was surveyed off the other. Bounded — an unbounded version lets a parcel slide along its neighbour collecting contact, which made results worse.

Optimisation is a deterministic compass (pattern) search on translation, wrapped in a rotation sweep only when orientation is genuinely unknown — a tippan with no stated base bearing. A traverse of true bearings already knows where north is, and rotating it would discard real information.

6.2 Positional uncertainty

Each placement is probed in eight directions to find how far the parcel can move before the fit measurably worsens — a parcel constrained on three sides cannot move; one with a single neighbour can slide. That local slack is then propagated along the inference chain, because a parcel placed off a parcel placed off the anchor inherits its parent's error, exactly as in any survey network. A calibration factor of 2.5 is applied to the local term, validated against ground truth so the figure over-reports rather than under-reports. An error bar that is confidently too small is worse than none.

Section 7

Validation and measured results

7.1 The ground-truth test

A village is constructed with known geometry, converted into documents containing only bearings, distances, areas and boundaries, every position but one is discarded, and the engine is asked to rebuild it. The reconstruction is then compared against the original. Run it with python tests/test_assembly_truth.py.

ScenarioParcelsAnchorsPlacedWorst centroid errorArea error
Regular grid, all traverse616/60.025 m0.000 m²
Irregular quadrilaterals, 2 rotation-unknown tippans818/8≈16 m<0.03 m²

Why the two numbers differ, and why that is honest rather than a defect

When parcels tile regularly the constraint network is over-determined — each parcel is hemmed in on several sides and only one placement satisfies everything, so the fit is centimetre-exact. Irregular quadrilaterals leave genuine slack: several placements satisfy "touches on the correct side" almost equally well. Shape and area remain exact in both cases; only position degrades. Rather than tune the optimiser until a synthetic case looks good, the system measures the slack and reports it per parcel.

8.1 Migration to PostGIS

The engine was written as a thin wrapper over the same predicates PostGIS exposes, so the port is mechanical rather than a rewrite:

-- area reconciliation                    -- overlapping ownership
SELECT survey_no,                          SELECT a.survey_no, b.survey_no,
       ST_Area(geom::geography)/10000             ST_Area(ST_Intersection(a.geom,b.geom)
         AS mapped_ha                                      ::geography)/10000 AS overlap_ha
FROM   parcels                             FROM   parcels a JOIN parcels b
WHERE  ABS(ST_Area(geom::geography)/10000         ON ST_Overlaps(a.geom, b.geom)
       - recorded_area_ha)                       AND a.id < b.id;
       / recorded_area_ha > 0.05;

-- parcel plotting outside its own village
SELECT p.survey_no FROM parcels p JOIN villages v USING (lgd_village_code)
WHERE  NOT ST_Within(p.geom, v.geom);

What this means for the demo

The claim that can be made honestly is: "Give us the measurements off any Indian land document and we will produce a validated, provenance-tracked polygon in WGS84, anywhere in the country, with an error bar. One anchored parcel maps an entire village." The claim that cannot yet be made is that OCR produces those measurements automatically, or that scanned cadastral map sheets are vectorised.

Two things to keep saying out loud

Geometry is a second validator

It is not a map viewer. A traverse that fails to close catches a dropped digit that no OCR confidence score can see, because the error is only visible when the measurements are required to be self-consistent. The register's declared area and the reconstructed area are two independent statements about one parcel; when they disagree, something on the page is wrong.

Nothing here changes a legal record

Indian land title is presumptive, not conclusive. An inferred position is a hypothesis for an officer to verify, never a statement about who owns what. That is precisely why the accuracy class, the provenance and the uncertainty travel with every parcel through the API and onto the map — so the two can never be confused.
